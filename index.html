Alcherogue

An alchemy shop game about pouring by eye.

There are no measurements. The liquid’s colour is the instrument, and the stream accelerates the longer you pour. Every sale asks the same question at a different scale: take the sure thing, or reach for something better?

Game design · Tactile systems · Economy · Perception · Prototyping

Read the design process

The system

Recipes name ingredients but never reveal their proportions. Potion quality is graded rather than passed or failed, so near misses still sell for less. Rent is due every third day and keeps rising, while stock purchases, haggling, and auctions make each coin a decision.

Pouring and haggling share one hold-and-release input with an accelerating cost. The same learned skill applies to both liquid and money.

Two designs I cut

The first version was a quiz roguelite. After nine versions, I cut it because a question becomes spent once its answer is known; adding more questions increased content, not depth.

The replacement was a deduction game with scrambled jar labels. A short Python script proved that every four-jar arrangement could be solved in exactly two pours with the same strategy. Random labels changed the instance, but not the pattern, so I cut that version too.

What the numbers changed

  • Economy simulations exposed free stock, predictable shortages, and rent that arrived too late.
  • Precision varied by 3.1× between recipes, while visible hue feedback varied by 4.8×.
  • One recipe changed by only 2.8° of hue, making memory-based judgement unfair rather than difficult.
  • A browser prototype made accelerating flow feel clearly better than constant flow.

Design method

I combined playable prototypes, small verification scripts, perceptual testing, MDA, game-feel analysis, and comparison with Potion Craft, Potionomics, Recettear, Strange Horticulture, and Moonlighter. The final direction puts the difficulty in the player’s hands—the gap I found in the genre, and also its main risk for players who come to shop games to relax.

(Not) Very Disco

Friends want a high five. Pickpockets want your stuff.

Wait for each dancer to reveal themselves, then choose: friend, tap left; pickpocket, tap right. Faster crowds and shrinking reaction windows turn two inputs into a tense score chase.

Game design · Two-input interaction · Feedback · Accessibility

Very Disco Game Jam 2026 — theme: “Compact.”

Read the design notes

“Compact” shaped both the controls and the setting: one-touch play inside a crowded dance floor. Tapping too early, too late, or choosing the wrong person costs a life; correct reads build combo and score.

Visual, audio, and haptic feedback distinguish the two actions. Reduce Motion, separate BGM and SFX controls, and haptic settings keep that feedback adjustable.

Fish Together

Your phone is the fishing rod.

Cast with real motion, feel bites through haptics, and race friends as every catch makes the next one rarer, riskier, and more rewarding.

Game design · Core loop · Motion controls · Haptics · Multiplayer

Read the design notes

Each Pond is a temporary shared match lasting no more than 20 minutes. Players fish side by side, compete on a live leaderboard, and level up through catches. Each level offers temporary buffs that can compound into a powerful build.

Every Pond resets everyone to zero. That roguelite structure keeps sessions fair and makes the choices inside the current run matter. The loop is simple: cast → catch → level up → compound → repeat.

HellMinistrative

Hell has paperwork. You have a keyboard and no time.

Type the Devil’s requests while deciding which document will expire next. Timing, accuracy, and prioritization all feed the same pressure system.

Game design · Typing feel · Pressure systems · Priority management

Game Seed 2026 — theme: “So Absurd, It’s Cool.”

Read the design notes
  • Overheat: time and typing accuracy determine whether the level is won or lost.
  • CAPTCHA: an urgent event that raises heat 1.5× faster until solved.
  • Document priority: every request carries its own weight and disappears if ignored for too long.

Together, these systems make typing only half the challenge. The player must read the queue, choose what matters now, and stay accurate while the cost of delay rises.

Nimbus Harvest

A cozy farming game powered by the weather outside your window.

Your local forecast becomes the farm’s weather. Plant under real sunshine, let a real storm water the fields, and grow crops around the conditions they love.

Game design · System design · Live weather · Cozy progression

App Store release · One post-launch patch · Available in the EU

Read the design notes

Sun, clouds, rain, snow, wind, and heat are not decoration: they directly affect crop growth. Each crop prefers different weather, turning a familiar forecast into a planning system and connecting play to the world outside the screen.
