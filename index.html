Alcherogue

How close can you get when colour is your only measuring tool?

Alcherogue is an alchemy shop game about pouring by eye. Hold too long and the stream runs away from you. Stop too early and the potion may only be good enough. There are no numbers to trust—only the colour in the bottle and your own judgement.

Game design · Tactile systems · Economy · Perception · Prototyping

Read the design process

Why remove the measuring tools?

Recipes tell you which ingredients belong together, but never the proportions. Every potion lands somewhere on a quality ladder, so a near miss still sells—just for less. The result is not a memory test. It is a test of control and judgement.

That same risk follows you through the shop. Rent rises every third day. Stock, haggling, and auctions all ask one question: take the safe result now, or push for something better? Pouring and haggling even share the same hold-and-release input.

Why did I throw away two versions?

The first was a quiz roguelite. After nine versions, I realised every question became useless once its answer was known. More questions meant more content, but not more depth.

The second used scrambled jar labels. A short Python script proved that every four-jar arrangement could be solved in exactly two pours with one fixed strategy. The labels changed, but the thinking did not. So I cut that version too.

What did testing reveal?

  • Economy simulations exposed free stock, predictable shortages, and rent that arrived too late.
  • Precision varied by 3.1× between recipes, while visible hue feedback varied by 4.8×.
  • One recipe changed by only 2.8° of hue, making memory-based judgement unfair rather than difficult.
  • A browser prototype made accelerating flow feel clearly better than constant flow.

How did I find the final direction?

I used playable prototypes, verification scripts, perceptual testing, MDA, game-feel analysis, and genre comparison. Most alchemy shop games make crafting tactile but forgiving. Alcherogue puts the challenge directly in the player’s hands. That is its identity—and its biggest design risk.

(Not) Very Disco

Can you read the crowd before the beat gets ahead of you?

The dance floor is packed. Friends want a high five; pickpockets want your stuff. Wait for each person to reveal themselves, then choose: friend, tap left; pickpocket, tap right. Two inputs are enough—until the crowd speeds up.

Game design · Two-input interaction · Feedback · Accessibility

Very Disco Game Jam 2026 — theme: “Compact.”

Read the design notes

How does a two-button game stay tense?

“Compact” shaped both the controls and the setting: one-touch play on a crowded dance floor. A correct read builds combo and score. Acting too early, too late, or choosing the wrong person costs a life.

The challenge grows through faster arrivals and smaller reaction windows, not extra buttons. Visual, audio, and haptic feedback keep every result readable, while Reduce Motion and separate feedback settings keep it adjustable.

The Chronicles of Gilbert

What if every card had to be played while you were still moving?

Build a deck, enter the dungeon, then survive it in real time. Cards become combat skills, but a strong hand means nothing if you mistime the dodge or lose your position.

Game design · Combat systems · Deckbuilding · Encounter design

Built with Godot 4.

Read the design notes

Where does the strategy happen?

The design joins two rhythms: careful deck construction and immediate action. Your deck decides which tools you bring. Movement, positioning, and dodge-rolling decide whether you can use them. Strategy happens both before and during combat.

Fish Together

What if your phone actually felt like the fishing rod?

Cast with real motion. Feel the bite through haptics. Then race your friends to build the strongest run before the Pond closes. Every catch makes the next choice rarer, riskier, and more rewarding.

Game design · Core loop · Motion controls · Haptics · Multiplayer

Read the design notes

What changes in every Pond?

A Pond is a shared match lasting no more than 20 minutes. Players fish side by side, chase a live leaderboard, and level up through catches. Every level offers temporary buffs that can combine into a powerful build.

When the Pond ends, every build disappears. The reset keeps the next match fair and makes each decision inside the current run matter. The loop is simple: cast → catch → level up → compound → repeat.

HellMinistrative

How fast can you type when every document is about to expire?

Hell has paperwork, and the Devil wants it done now. Type each request accurately while deciding which document will disappear next. Speed helps—but choosing the wrong task first can still ruin the level.

Game design · Typing feel · Pressure systems · Priority management

Game Seed 2026 — theme: “So Absurd, It’s Cool.”

Read the design notes

Where does the pressure come from?

  • Overheat: time and typing accuracy determine whether the level is won or lost.
  • CAPTCHA: an urgent event that raises heat 1.5× faster until solved.
  • Document priority: every request carries its own weight and disappears if ignored for too long.

Typing is only half the challenge. The player must read the queue, decide what matters now, and stay accurate while every delay becomes more expensive.

Nimbus Harvest

What if today’s real weather decided what grew in your farm?

Nimbus Harvest turns your local forecast into a cozy farming system. Plant under real sunshine, let a real storm water the fields, and choose crops around the weather happening outside your window.

Game design · System design · Live weather · Cozy progression

App Store release · One post-launch patch · Available in the EU

Read the design notes

Why make real weather part of the game?

Sun, clouds, rain, snow, wind, and heat directly affect crop growth. Each crop prefers different conditions, so the forecast becomes a planning tool rather than decoration. The goal was to make checking the real sky feel like part of play.
